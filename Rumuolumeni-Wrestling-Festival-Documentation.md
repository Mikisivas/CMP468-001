# DOCUMENTATION OF RUMUOLUMENI WRESTLING FESTIVAL: A MIXED MEDIA EXPERIMENTATION WITH TISSUE PAPER AND PIGMENT ON CANVAS

---

## ABSTRACT

This study documents the Rumuolumeni wrestling festival through studio practice, using mixed media experimentation with tissue paper and pigment on canvas. Rumuolumeni, a community in the Obio/Akpor Local Government Area of Rivers State, holds traditional wrestling as a marker of strength, communal identity, and seasonal celebration. Rapid urbanisation around Port Harcourt has thinned the practice of this festival, and few visual records preserve its movement, costume, and crowd energy. The work addresses that gap. The researcher observed the festival, collected photographs and oral accounts, then translated the gathered material into a body of canvas works. Tissue paper supplies relief, folds, and torn edges that read as muscle, cloth, and dust. Pigment supplies colour, tone, and the heat of the arena. The study produced a series of studio pieces, each built from direct observation and layered construction. Findings show that tissue paper and pigment, used together, carry both the texture and the emotion of a wrestling bout better than flat paint alone. The study recommends that cultural festivals in the Niger Delta be documented through studio art as a parallel record to photography and text.

# CHAPTER ONE: INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Background to the Study

Traditional wrestling sits at the centre of social life for many communities across the Niger Delta. Among the Ikwerre people of Rivers State, the contest is more than sport. A match tests the body, settles rank among young men, and gives a village a shared reason to gather. Rumuolumeni, one of the Ikwerre settlements within the Obio/Akpor Local Government Area, keeps this custom through a seasonal wrestling festival. Drummers set the rhythm. Elders ring the arena. Wrestlers enter with chalk and oil on their skin, and the crowd presses close enough to feel each throw.

A festival of this kind carries layers of meaning. The costume signals lineage. The dance before the grip honours ancestors. The win raises a family name. These layers live in motion and sound, which fade the moment the drumming stops. Photography freezes a single instant. Written reports fix names and dates. Neither form holds the full weight of colour, texture, and bodily tension that a spectator feels inside the ring.

Painting offers a different kind of record. A canvas lets an artist combine many moments into one surface, select what matters, and press emotion into material. Mixed media widens that reach. When an artist moves past a single medium, the surface gains depth that flat pigment cannot reach on its own. Tissue paper, cheap and common, folds and tears into ridges that catch light. Layered and sealed, it becomes a skin on the canvas that mimics muscle, wrapper cloth, and the raised dust of the arena floor. Pigment laid over and into that skin supplies the brown of oiled bodies, the red of festival cloth, and the ochre of trodden ground.

This study joins the subject and the method. It documents the Rumuolumeni wrestling festival and, at the same time, tests how far tissue paper and pigment can carry the life of that festival on canvas. The work treats the studio as a site of research, where the act of making produces knowledge about both the culture and the medium.

## 1.2 Statement of the Problem

The Rumuolumeni wrestling festival is losing ground. Port Harcourt has grown outward, and communities that once set aside full seasons for wrestling now hold shorter, thinner events. Young people migrate for school and work. Formal sport competes with the village arena for their attention. As the practice shrinks, so does the body of knowledge tied to it, the chants, the costume rules, the order of bouts.

Existing records of the festival are scarce and mostly verbal. Few photographs circulate beyond family phones. Scholarly writing on Ikwerre wrestling is limited, and almost none of it treats the festival as subject matter for studio art. Where visual art in the region does engage culture, it leans on conventional painting that flattens the texture and the physical charge of the event.

Two gaps follow. First, the festival lacks a durable visual archive built by an artist who watched it closely. Second, the expressive potential of tissue paper and pigment, as a combined medium for rendering cultural movement, remains largely untested in this context. This study responds to both gaps by producing a documented body of mixed media works grounded in the Rumuolumeni festival.

## 1.3 Aim and Objectives of the Study

The aim of this study is to document the Rumuolumeni wrestling festival through a body of mixed media studio works built from tissue paper and pigment on canvas.

The objectives are to:

1. Observe and record the key stages, costumes, and movements of the Rumuolumeni wrestling festival through field study.
2. Explore the working properties of tissue paper and pigment as a combined medium on canvas.
3. Translate the gathered festival material into a series of studio compositions.
4. Analyse the finished works for how well the medium conveys the texture, motion, and emotion of the festival.
5. Establish studio art as a method for documenting cultural festivals in the Niger Delta.

## 1.4 Research Questions

1. What are the main stages, costumes, and movements that define the Rumuolumeni wrestling festival?
2. What working properties do tissue paper and pigment show when combined on canvas?
3. How can festival material be translated into mixed media studio compositions?
4. How well does the combined medium convey the texture, motion, and emotion of the festival?
5. In what ways can studio art serve as a record of cultural festivals in the Niger Delta?

## 1.5 Significance of the Study

This study serves several groups.

For the Rumuolumeni community, the work builds a visual archive of a festival under pressure. The finished pieces hold the costumes, postures, and arena setting in a form that outlasts the single day of the event.

For art students and studio practitioners, the study offers a tested account of how tissue paper and pigment behave together. Readers gain a practical guide to layering, sealing, and tearing the material for relief and tone.

For scholars of Ikwerre and Niger Delta culture, the study adds a visual source to a thin written record. The documentation supports later research on wrestling, festival dress, and communal ritual.

For museums and cultural bodies in Rivers State, the body of work provides exhibition material that connects local heritage to contemporary studio practice.

## 1.6 Scope and Delimitation of the Study

The study covers the Rumuolumeni wrestling festival as observed in the Obio/Akpor Local Government Area of Rivers State. It focuses on the visual elements of the festival, costume, posture, movement, arena, and crowd, rather than on its full historical or anthropological record.

In the studio, the work is limited to mixed media on canvas, with tissue paper and pigment as the primary materials. Other media enter only as support, such as adhesive, gesso, and sealant. The study does not extend to sculpture, printmaking, or digital art. The analysis treats the works produced within the project period and does not survey the wider body of Niger Delta festival art.

## 1.7 Definition of Terms

**Mixed media.** An art form that combines two or more materials or techniques on a single surface. In this study, the term refers to tissue paper and pigment worked together on canvas.

**Tissue paper.** A thin, soft, lightweight paper. In this study, tissue paper is folded, torn, and layered on canvas to build relief and texture.

**Pigment.** A dry or bound colouring material applied to a surface. In this study, the term covers the acrylic and poster colour used to tone and finish the works.

**Documentation.** The act of recording an event or practice in a durable form. Here, documentation takes place through studio art rather than through photography or text alone.

**Wrestling festival.** A seasonal communal event in which young men contest through traditional wrestling, framed by drumming, dance, and gathering.

**Rumuolumeni.** An Ikwerre community in the Obio/Akpor Local Government Area of Rivers State, and the setting of the festival studied here.

**Relief.** A raised surface. In this study, relief is built from layered tissue paper so that parts of the composition stand above the plane of the canvas.

# CHAPTER TWO: REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

## 2.1 Introduction

This chapter reviews writing and studio practice tied to the study. The review moves through five parts. It opens with a conceptual frame for the key ideas. It then treats traditional wrestling as cultural practice, the documentation of festivals through visual art, the use of mixed media in painting, and the specific behaviour of tissue paper and pigment. The chapter closes with the theoretical frame that guides the studio work and a short summary of the gap the study fills.

## 2.2 Conceptual Framework

Three concepts anchor the study: cultural documentation, mixed media, and texture as meaning.

Cultural documentation treats art as a carrier of memory. A festival that lives in motion needs a vessel once the motion stops. Writers on African art have long held that the visual object stores communal knowledge, from masquerade carving to wall painting. The artist who documents a festival does not copy it. The artist selects, orders, and heightens, and in doing so keeps the meaning rather than the mere appearance.

Mixed media names the practice of building one surface from several materials. The concept rests on the idea that each material brings a quality no single medium holds alone. The artist who reaches for mixed media seeks range: weight next to lightness, rough next to smooth, raised next to flat.

Texture as meaning holds that a surface quality carries emotion and reference on its own, apart from image. A torn, ridged surface reads as struggle before the eye reads any figure. This concept drives the choice of tissue paper, whose folds and tears speak to the strain of a wrestling bout.

## 2.3 Traditional Wrestling as Cultural Practice

Across West Africa, wrestling serves as contest, rite, and spectacle. Among the Igbo and the related Ikwerre, wrestling marks the passage of young men into full standing in the community. Writers on Igbo culture describe the wrestling ground as a stage where strength, skill, and courage earn public name. The famous opening of Chinua Achebe's account of Umuofia, where Okonkwo throws Amalinze the Cat, shows how deeply the contest sits in the cultural memory of the region.

For the Ikwerre of Rivers State, wrestling ties to the farming calendar and to communal festivals that gather scattered kin. The bout carries ritual weight. Chalk, oil, and charm prepare the body. Drumming and chant frame the match. The winner raises not only himself but his lineage. These accounts establish wrestling as subject matter rich in colour, movement, and meaning, well suited to studio documentation.

The literature, though, treats wrestling mostly through text and oral record. Visual study of the Rumuolumeni festival in particular stays thin. This absence supports the present study.

## 2.4 Documentation of Festivals Through Visual Art

Visual art has long recorded African festivals. Masquerade, shrine painting, and body art each preserve ritual knowledge in material form. Modern Nigerian artists have carried this task onto canvas. The Osogbo artists rendered Yoruba myth and festival in paint and print. Bruce Onobrakpeya built prints and relief works around Urhobo ritual and folklore. Ben Enwonwu set Igbo masquerade and dance into sculpture and painting that reached national audiences.

These bodies of work show a steady principle. The artist who documents a festival works from close knowledge, then recasts the event through a personal medium. The record gains durability and gains a point of view. The present study follows this line, turning the Rumuolumeni wrestling festival into studio documentation through mixed media.

Yet the existing documentation leans on conventional media, oil, print, bronze. The textured, relief approach that tissue paper allows stays under-used for festival subjects. The study addresses that opening.

## 2.5 Mixed Media in Contemporary Painting

Mixed media widened the field of modern art. Early collage by Picasso and Braque placed printed paper and found material onto the picture plane and broke the single-medium habit. Later artists pushed the surface further. Robert Rauschenberg built combines from paint and object. Nigerian artists absorbed and reshaped the approach. Uche Okeke, Nsukka's leading voice, joined drawing idiom with varied surface. Jimoh Akolo and others folded local material into painted work.

Writers on contemporary African art note that mixed media suits a setting where materials are near at hand and meaning is layered. The practice lets an artist join found and formal material, and lets the surface itself speak. For a wrestling festival, where bodies strain and cloth and dust fill the air, a built surface carries more than a smooth one.

## 2.6 Tissue Paper as an Art Medium

Tissue paper enters studio practice as a cheap, pliable material. Its thinness lets light pass and lets colour sit soft. Its weakness, when torn, gives ragged edges that read as organic. Layered and sealed, tissue builds a low relief that holds shadow.

Craft and studio sources treat tissue paper for collage, for surface texture, and for translucent colour fields. In a classroom and studio setting, the material is valued because it responds to the hand without special tools. An artist can crumple, fold, twist, and tear tissue, then fix it with adhesive and seal it for strength. These properties make tissue paper a strong candidate for rendering the folds of festival cloth, the swell of muscle, and the churn of arena dust.

The literature, though, treats tissue paper mostly in craft and beginner contexts. Its use as a serious medium for cultural documentation on canvas stays rare. The study tests the material in that more demanding role.

## 2.7 Pigment and Colour in Studio Practice

Pigment carries colour, tone, and mood. In studio painting, the choice and handling of pigment shape how a work reads. Warm pigments, the browns, reds, and ochres, raise heat and energy. These tones match the oiled skin, the festival cloth, and the sun-struck ground of a wrestling arena.

Acrylic and poster pigments suit mixed media work with tissue paper. Acrylic binds well to a sealed paper surface, dries fast, and layers without lifting the paper below. Poster colour supplies flat, bright fields for cloth and chalk marks. Sources on painting technique stress that pigment laid over a textured ground behaves differently from pigment on a flat primed surface. The raised tissue catches and holds colour at its ridges and lets thinner washes pool in its hollows. This interaction between pigment and relief is central to the study.

## 2.8 Theoretical Framework

The study draws on two theories.

The first is the Natural Synthesis theory advanced by Uche Okeke and the Zaria Art Society. Natural Synthesis calls on the Nigerian artist to blend indigenous content and form with studio techniques learned from wider practice. The theory supports a work that takes a local festival as content and renders it through a modern mixed media method. The study is, in method and subject, an act of synthesis.

The second is the formalist view that surface qualities, texture, line, colour, and mass, carry meaning in themselves. This view guides the handling of tissue paper and pigment, where the raised, torn surface is asked to express strain and energy apart from the depicted figures. Together the two theories frame a study that is local in subject and material-driven in method.

## 2.9 Summary of Literature Reviewed

The review shows traditional wrestling as a rich cultural practice held mostly in text and oral record, with thin visual study of the Rumuolumeni festival. It shows a strong tradition of documenting African festivals through visual art, carried out largely in conventional media. It shows mixed media as an established and well-suited approach for layered cultural subjects. It shows tissue paper and pigment as materials with clear expressive potential, though tissue paper remains tied to craft rather than serious documentation.

The gap is now clear. No existing body of work documents the Rumuolumeni wrestling festival through mixed media experimentation with tissue paper and pigment on canvas. The present study fills that gap.

# CHAPTER THREE: MATERIALS AND METHODS

## 3.1 Introduction

This chapter sets out how the study was carried out. It covers the research design, the field study for data gathering, the materials and tools, and the step-by-step studio process. The studio process forms the core method, since the study produces knowledge through making.

## 3.2 Research Design

The study uses a studio-based practice-led design, supported by field observation. Practice-led research treats the act of making as the main route to knowledge. The studio work does not illustrate a conclusion reached elsewhere. The work tests the research questions directly, through material trial and finished composition.

Field observation supports the studio. The researcher watched the festival, gathered visual and oral material, then carried that material into the studio. The two parts feed each other. Observation supplies content. Studio trial supplies form.

## 3.3 Sources of Data

Data came from primary and secondary sources.

Primary data came from direct observation of the Rumuolumeni wrestling festival, from photographs taken during the event, from sketches made on site and from memory, and from informal talks with elders, drummers, and wrestlers about costume, order of bouts, and meaning.

Secondary data came from books, journals, and studio sources on Ikwerre culture, traditional wrestling, mixed media practice, and the handling of tissue paper and pigment. These sources shaped the review and guided the studio choices.

## 3.4 Field Study and Data Gathering

The field study ran in stages. The researcher first made contact with the community and sought leave to observe and record. During the festival, the researcher took a position near the arena and recorded the event in photographs and quick sketches. Attention fell on the entry of wrestlers, the pre-bout dance, the grip and throw, the drummers, and the ring of spectators.

After the event, the researcher held short talks with willing elders and wrestlers to confirm the names and meanings of costumes and moves. The photographs and sketches were then sorted into themes: single figures, locked pairs, the drumming line, and the crowd. These themes became the basis for the studio compositions.

## 3.5 Materials and Tools

The study used the following materials:

- **Canvas.** Stretched cotton canvas in several sizes served as the support.
- **Tissue paper.** Plain white and coloured tissue paper supplied relief and soft colour.
- **Pigment.** Acrylic colour and poster colour supplied tone, with a warm range of browns, reds, ochres, and black.
- **Adhesive.** White glue, thinned with water, fixed the tissue paper to the canvas.
- **Gesso.** White gesso primed the canvas and sealed layers between stages.
- **Sealant.** A clear acrylic medium sealed and strengthened the finished surface.

The study used the following tools:

- Brushes of several widths for glue, pigment, and sealant.
- A palette knife for pressing and shaping the tissue relief.
- Sponges and rags for lifting and blending pigment.
- A spray bottle of water for softening tissue during shaping.
- Pencil and charcoal for the first drawing on the primed canvas.

## 3.6 Studio Procedure

The studio work followed a set sequence, repeated for each piece.

**Step one: priming.** The stretched canvas received a coat of gesso and was left to dry. The primed ground gave the adhesive and pigment a stable surface.

**Step two: drawing.** The chosen composition was drawn onto the primed canvas in pencil or light charcoal. The drawing fixed the placement of figures, drummers, and crowd.

**Step three: building relief.** Tissue paper was torn, folded, and crumpled, then set into the drawn areas with thinned glue. Thicker layers built the muscles and raised cloth. Thin, smooth layers covered the ground and sky. The palette knife and fingers pressed the tissue into ridges and hollows while the glue stayed wet.

**Step four: sealing the relief.** Once the tissue dried, a coat of thinned glue or gesso sealed the surface. The seal strengthened the paper and prepared it to take pigment without tearing.

**Step five: colouring.** Pigment went on in stages. Thin washes laid the base tone and sank into the hollows of the relief. Thicker colour picked out the ridges, the oiled skin, and the bright festival cloth. Dry brush and sponge work raised the texture of dust and chalk.

**Step six: detailing and finishing.** Fine brushwork added faces, charms, and the lines of strain. A clear acrylic medium sealed the finished piece and unified the surface sheen.

## 3.7 Method of Data Analysis

The finished works were analysed through formal description and interpretation. Each piece was examined for its handling of texture, movement, colour, and composition, and for how well the medium carried the chosen festival moment. The analysis judged the works against the research questions, in particular the capacity of tissue paper and pigment to convey the texture, motion, and emotion of the festival. This qualitative, studio-based analysis appears in Chapter Four.

# CHAPTER FOUR: STUDIO PRACTICE, ANALYSIS, AND DISCUSSION

## 4.1 Introduction

This chapter presents the studio outcomes of the study. It reports the material trials that opened the work, describes and analyses the main pieces produced, and discusses what the body of work shows about the medium and the subject. The analysis answers the research questions through the finished works.

## 4.2 Preliminary Material Trials

Before the main compositions, the researcher ran trials to learn the behaviour of tissue paper and pigment together.

The first trial tested layering. Single sheets of tissue gave weak, flat texture. Three to five crumpled layers gave firm relief that held shadow and survived later brushwork. The trial set the working rule: build muscle and cloth from several layers, keep ground and sky to one or two.

The second trial tested sealing. Pigment laid on unsealed tissue lifted and tore the paper. A seal of thinned glue or gesso fixed the problem and let the pigment sit and layer. The trial set sealing as a required step between relief and colour.

The third trial tested pigment on relief. Thin washes pooled in the hollows and left the ridges pale, which read as light falling across a raised form. Thick colour on the ridges raised the sense of muscle and cloth. The trial confirmed that the relief and the pigment work as one system, not as two separate stages.

These trials answered the second research question directly. Tissue paper and pigment, combined on canvas, show clear and workable properties: layered relief, a required seal, and a pigment response keyed to the raised surface.

## 4.3 Description and Analysis of the Studio Works

The study produced a series of canvas works. The main pieces are described and analysed below. Each title names the festival moment it documents.

### 4.3.1 Plate I: "Entry of the Wrestlers"

This piece documents the opening procession, when wrestlers enter the arena. The composition sets three advancing figures against a textured ground of crowd and dust. The figures rise in heavy tissue relief, their oiled torsos built from layered, crumpled paper that catches warm brown and ochre pigment. The crowd behind them sits in flatter, cooler tissue, pushed back by thinner colour.

The analysis shows the medium carrying the swagger of the entry. The raised figures step forward out of the plane, and the pooled pigment in the hollows of the relief reads as the shadow of strong afternoon light. The torn edges of the tissue at the wrappers give the cloth a living, wind-caught look. The piece answers the question of texture and motion in the affirmative.

### 4.3.2 Plate II: "The Grip"

This piece documents the locked pair at the centre of a bout. Two figures bend into each other, arms clasped, weight pressed low. The relief here reaches its heaviest. The researcher built the strained backs and shoulders from the thickest tissue layers in the series, so that the muscle stands well above the plane.

The analysis shows texture working as emotion. The ridged, tense surface of the locked bodies reads as strain before the eye sorts out the two figures. Warm pigment on the ridges and dark pigment in the deep hollows raise the sense of effort. The torn tissue at the point of contact blurs where one body ends and the other begins, which matches the real confusion of a close grip. The piece is the strongest proof in the series that texture carries meaning on its own.

### 4.3.3 Plate III: "The Throw"

This piece documents the climax, when one wrestler lifts and throws the other. The composition tilts on a diagonal to carry the fall. The thrown figure arcs across the upper canvas in lighter relief, while the standing victor holds the lower ground in heavy relief. Dust rises between them in dry-brushed ochre over lightly crumpled tissue.

The analysis shows the medium handling motion. The diagonal and the lighter relief of the falling figure give a sense of weightlessness at the top of the throw. The dust, built from loose tissue and broken pigment, fills the gap with movement. The piece answers the question of motion, though the frozen single moment of a throw tests the limits of a static medium.

### 4.3.4 Plate IV: "The Drummers"

This piece documents the drumming line that frames the festival. A row of seated drummers works across the lower canvas, hands caught mid-beat. The relief is moderate, raised at the hands and drum heads, flatter at the bodies. The pigment runs warm at the drums and cooler at the seated forms.

The analysis shows the medium handling rhythm and setting. The repeated raised drum heads set a visual beat across the canvas that stands in for sound. The piece documents a part of the festival often left out of single-figure records, and it widens the archive the study builds.

### 4.3.5 Plate V: "The Arena"

This piece documents the full scene, the ring of spectators around a central bout. The composition is the largest and busiest in the series. Heavy relief marks the central wrestlers. The ringing crowd recedes in bands of lighter, cooler, flatter tissue. Warm pigment holds the centre, and cool pigment pushes the edges back.

The analysis shows the medium handling space and crowd. The step from heavy, warm relief at the centre to thin, cool, flat tissue at the edge builds depth without losing the energy of the throng. The piece gathers the themes of the series into one surface and serves as the summary document of the festival.

## 4.4 Discussion of Findings

The body of work answers the research questions.

On the stages, costumes, and movements of the festival, the series documents the entry, the grip, the throw, the drumming, and the full arena. Together the pieces hold the visual order of the event.

On the properties of the combined medium, the trials and the finished works show that tissue paper builds workable relief, that a seal is required between relief and colour, and that pigment responds to the raised surface in a way that reads as light and form.

On the translation of festival material into composition, the field photographs and sketches moved into studio pieces through the set procedure. The study shows a clear, repeatable route from observation to canvas.

On how well the medium conveys texture, motion, and emotion, the works show strong results for texture and emotion, best seen in "The Grip," and good but more limited results for motion, where a static surface meets a fast event.

On studio art as a record of cultural festivals, the finished series stands as a durable visual archive of the Rumuolumeni wrestling festival, built by an artist who watched it closely. The study shows that studio art can serve as a record that holds colour, texture, and feeling beyond the reach of photograph or text alone.

The findings also raise a point for practice. Tissue paper, often held to be a craft material, carries serious documentary work when layered, sealed, and worked with pigment. The study lifts the material into a more demanding role and shows that the role suits it.

# CHAPTER FIVE: SUMMARY, CONCLUSION, AND RECOMMENDATIONS

## 5.1 Summary

This study set out to document the Rumuolumeni wrestling festival through a body of mixed media studio works built from tissue paper and pigment on canvas. The study grew from two concerns. The festival is losing ground to urban growth and migration, and it holds no durable visual archive built by a close observer. At the same time, the expressive reach of tissue paper and pigment, as a combined medium for cultural movement, had not been tested in this setting.

The study used a practice-led design supported by field observation. The researcher watched the festival, gathered photographs, sketches, and oral accounts, then carried the material into the studio. A set procedure guided each piece: prime, draw, build relief from layered tissue, seal, colour with pigment keyed to the relief, then detail and finish.

Material trials opened the work and set the working rules on layering, sealing, and pigment response. The study then produced a series of canvas pieces that document the entry, the grip, the throw, the drumming, and the full arena. Analysis of the works showed strong handling of texture and emotion, good handling of motion, and a clear capacity to serve as a visual record of the festival.

## 5.2 Conclusion

The study reached its aim. It produced a documented body of mixed media works grounded in the Rumuolumeni wrestling festival, and in doing so it tested and proved the combined medium.

Three conclusions follow. First, tissue paper and pigment, worked together on canvas, carry the texture and the emotion of a wrestling bout better than flat paint alone. The raised, torn surface speaks of strain and effort before the eye reads any figure. Second, the studio can serve as a site of cultural documentation. A festival that lives in motion and sound finds a durable second life on a built surface, held by an artist who watched it closely. Third, tissue paper, long tied to craft, carries serious documentary work when it is layered, sealed, and worked with pigment.

The study joins a local subject and a material-driven method, in the spirit of Natural Synthesis. It adds a visual source to a thin record of Ikwerre culture, and it offers studio practitioners a tested account of a cheap and ready medium.

## 5.3 Recommendations

Based on the study, the following recommendations are made:

1. Cultural festivals in the Niger Delta should be documented through studio art as a parallel record to photography and text, so that colour, texture, and feeling are preserved alongside fact.
2. Art departments should treat tissue paper as a serious medium, not only a craft material, and should teach its layering, sealing, and pigment handling as a route to relief and texture on canvas.
3. The Rumuolumeni community and local cultural bodies should support the recording of the wrestling festival, through access and oral knowledge, before further loss of the practice.
4. Museums and galleries in Rivers State should exhibit mixed media documentation of local festivals, to tie heritage to contemporary studio practice and to widen public interest in the custom.
5. Further studio research should test the same medium on other Niger Delta festivals, such as masquerade and boat regatta, and should explore ways to carry fast motion more fully on a static surface.

## 5.4 Contribution to Knowledge

The study makes three contributions. It builds the first studio-based visual archive of the Rumuolumeni wrestling festival. It establishes a tested procedure for combining tissue paper and pigment on canvas for cultural documentation. It raises tissue paper from a craft material to a documentary medium within the practice of Niger Delta studio art.
